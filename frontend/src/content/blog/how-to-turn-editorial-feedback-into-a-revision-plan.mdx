Editorial feedback rarely arrives in the order a novel should be revised. A letter discusses character, then pacing, then returns to character from another angle. Margin comments identify symptoms scene by scene. A call introduces a possible solution that affects five chapters. If you begin at page one and address notes as encountered, you may polish effects before changing their causes.

Asha receives feedback on a courtroom fantasy. The editor says the middle feels repetitive, the accused prince is passive, the magical oath rules are unclear, and the final confession lacks impact. Margin notes ask for stronger stakes in four hearings. Asha creates forty-seven tasks. Most say “add tension.” None identifies what tension should come from.

A revision plan is not a cleaned-up comment list. It is a model of the underlying story problems, the decisions that resolve them, and the order in which those decisions propagate through the manuscript.

## Read everything before editing anything

Read the editorial letter once for the whole argument. Read again with the manuscript comments. Do not defend, accept, or rewrite yet. Mark emotional reactions separately so they do not masquerade as craft conclusions.

Then summarize the feedback in your own words. If you cannot explain a note without quoting it, you may not understand the concern. Questions for the editor should seek clarity: “When you call the prince passive, do you mean he lacks a goal in the hearings or that his choices do not alter their outcomes?”

Give strong reactions time. A note can feel wrong because it proposes the wrong solution while accurately identifying a reader experience.

Keep the original notes intact. Your interpretation should sit beside them, not replace them.

## Separate observation, diagnosis, and suggestion

Editorial notes often contain three layers:

- **observation:** what the reader experienced;
- **diagnosis:** why the editor thinks it happened;
- **suggestion:** one possible change.

“The second hearing drags” is observation. “Because the prince has no strategy” is diagnosis. “Have him accuse the judge” is suggestion.

Treat observations as evidence, diagnoses as hypotheses, and suggestions as options. You may reject the accusation while accepting that the prince needs an active strategy. This separation protects both author ownership and the value of feedback.

Margin notes such as “confusing” are observations with a location. Investigate what model the reader had before that line and what information disrupted it.

## Cluster notes by underlying problem

Copy or reference every substantial note in a feedback log. Group notes that may share a cause. Asha’s repetitive hearings, passive prince, and weak confession may all stem from the prince using the same defensive strategy while evidence changes around him.

Useful clusters include protagonist agency, antagonist pressure, relationship progression, world rule, chronology, viewpoint, setup/payoff, scene function, and ending consequence. Use categories that describe this manuscript, not a universal template.

Name each cluster as a problem statement:

> Across hearings two through five, the prince reacts to accusations but does not pursue a changing objective, so new evidence does not produce strategic escalation.

That statement is more actionable than “fix pacing.” It links reader experience to a causal pattern.

## Find the earliest cause in the manuscript

For each cluster, locate where the current story condition begins, not where the editor first comments on it.

The final confession lacks impact partly because the oath rules are unclear in Chapter 3 and because the prince has not previously risked speech under oath. Expanding the confession would address the endpoint while leaving preparation weak.

Write an origin scene and downstream range for every workstream. Earlier causes usually receive priority because later scenes depend on them.

Some feedback reveals an absent cause. If a friendship rupture seems abrupt, the earliest repair may be a missing pattern of reliance rather than a faulty rupture scene.

## Make story decisions before scene tasks

Do not write “revise Chapter 12” until you decide what becomes true. Asha must choose the oath system’s limits, the prince’s actual strategy, and what the confession costs.

Create a decision record:

- issue;
- current manuscript state;
- chosen new state;
- reason;
- scenes that establish it;
- scenes that depend on it;
- questions still open.

For example: the oath punishes deliberate falsehood with loss of one spoken memory, not physical pain. The prince can evade through silence but loses legal standing. His strategy becomes forcing prosecutors to state claims under the same oath.

Now scene tasks can be precise.

## Order workstreams by dependency

Structural revision has an order. World rules affect available choices. Character objectives affect scene turns. Scene order affects transitions. Prose polish comes later.

A practical sequence is:

1. governing premise, ending, and non-negotiable decisions;
2. chronology, world rules, and information access;
3. protagonist and antagonist objectives;
4. plot and subplot causal structure;
5. relationship and emotional progression;
6. scene purpose, pacing, and viewpoint;
7. continuity verification;
8. line-level voice and clarity.

Your novel may differ, but draw the dependency. Do not perfect banter in a hearing likely to be cut.

The best task order follows what later pages depend on, not the order in which comments appear or the order of chapters.

## Translate clusters into bounded passes

A pass needs a question, scope, evidence, and completion condition. “Character pass” is too broad. “Across the five hearings, give the prince a distinct objective and strategy that changes after each result” is bounded.

For each pass, list scenes to inspect, fields to track, edits permitted, and what remains out of scope. This reduces cognitive overload and prevents line polishing during structural work.

Asha’s oath-rule pass covers every magical oath use, explanation, evasion, cost, and witness. It does not yet rewrite courtroom dialogue except where necessary to establish function.

Limit the active queue. Keep later tasks visible but not open. Revision becomes calmer when only one causal system is under examination.

## Test proposed solutions against the novel’s aims

Before committing to an editor’s suggestion or your own, ask whether it preserves genre, viewpoint, tone, character agency, and the experience you want readers to have.

Having the prince accuse the judge might create activity but turn a story about strategic restraint into rebellion. A better solution may let him use the oath system to make the prosecutor risk memory.

Generate at least two options for high-impact problems. Compare consequences, not cleverness. Which scenes change? What new setup is required? What thematic meaning follows? What future problem does the option create?

Keep rejected options briefly with reasons. This prevents cycling back to a choice you already tested.

## Use scene cards to plan propagation

Once decisions are firm, map affected scenes. Mark establish, develop, complicate, turn, payoff, and aftermath. Add continuity dependencies such as who knows the oath’s true cost.

Do not draft every new scene before seeing the sequence. A card may reveal that two proposed additions perform the same function or that an existing hearing can carry the new turn.

For changed facts, search the manuscript globally. The old version may survive in narration, dialogue, metaphor, and character behaviour even when the direct explanation changes.

Update the story bible after the manuscript, not instead of it.

## Track notes without obeying them mechanically

Give each original note a status: addressed by workstream, accepted separately, declined with reason, needs clarification, or revisit after reread. Link it to the decision or pass that handles it.

Several notes may be resolved by one structural change. Do not add a separate patch for each. Conversely, one broad note may require many edits.

Declining a note is a decision, not a failure. Record why: conflicts with core aim, based on intentional ambiguity, contradicted by other evidence, or solved through another approach. Then check whether the reader experience remains and whether you accept it.

## Draft from the center of change outward

For a rewritten sequence, start with the scene where the new decision becomes most concrete. This can clarify what earlier setup and later consequence actually need.

Asha rewrites the fourth hearing, where the prince first forces the prosecutor under oath. She then revises earlier hearings to prepare his learning and later scenes to carry the political cost. This is different from random order because the work still follows a mapped dependency.

When changes are extensive, use temporary scene versions rather than overwriting every original immediately. Preserve recoverable history without leaving duplicate alternatives in the final manuscript.

## Keep a revision risk register

Large changes create predictable risks. A new motive can break chronology, shift sympathy, duplicate another character’s role, or make a later choice unnecessary. List those risks beside each high-impact decision before drafting.

For the oath revision, Asha notes four risks: earlier trials may become legally implausible, the prince may appear too knowledgeable too soon, the prosecutor may lose competence, and the confession may no longer be the only way to expose the conspiracy. Each risk becomes a verification question rather than a reason to avoid change.

Add evidence that will close the risk: a rule audit, knowledge map, antagonist pass, or alternative-ending test. This keeps revision from solving the editor’s note while quietly producing another structural weakness.

Risk registers should remain small. Track consequences that would materially damage the story, not every uncertain sentence.

For uncertain solutions, design a reversible test. Rewrite one representative hearing, draft two versions of the prince’s strategy, or outline the changed ending before propagating edits across the manuscript. Define what the experiment should reveal and compare it against the workstream’s problem statement.

An experiment is not avoidance when it leads to a decision. Archive rejected versions, record why the chosen one works better, and then commit. This protects time without leaving the final draft full of parallel possibilities.

## Verify with continuous reading

After each workstream, read from before its first change through after its last consequence. Check causality, knowledge, emotional continuity, and repeated explanation. A pass can be internally correct while damaging rhythm with surrounding material.

Then read the whole manuscript after structural passes converge. Look for seams between revised and untouched prose, character knowledge from old versions, and setup that now points elsewhere.

Use fresh readers after you can no longer distinguish plan from page. Ask about the original reader experience rather than announcing the repair.

## Close the loop with the editor when appropriate

If follow-up is part of the service, bring a concise list of decisions and genuine questions. Do not ask the editor to approve every choice. Ask where your interpretation of a note may be incomplete and which risks they see in the plan.

If no follow-up is included, your log still preserves the dialogue. You can evaluate the next draft against the feedback’s underlying observations.

In Asha’s revised novel, the number of hearings decreases from five to four. The prince changes strategy after each result. The oath cost is established through action, and the final confession sacrifices a memory readers have seen him protect. One causal plan resolves notes about passivity, repetition, rules, and payoff without forty-seven unrelated fixes.

Editorial feedback becomes useful when it stops being a verdict and becomes evidence. Understand the experience, test the diagnosis, choose the story’s new truth, and revise in the order that truth travels.
