A continuity error is rarely born as a spectacular plot hole. More often, it begins as a reasonable sentence in an earlier version of the book. A journey takes two days. A character locks a door. A winter storm closes the pass. Then revision changes the surrounding scenes. The journey now starts a day later, the key has moved to another character, and the storm has become early spring rain. Each sentence still reads cleanly. Together, they no longer describe the same story.

That is why continuity work cannot be reduced to proofreading. Grammar tools can tell you whether a sentence is formed correctly; they cannot reliably tell you whether the sentence agrees with a fact established eight chapters earlier. Continuity is a story-level discipline. It asks whether time, knowledge, bodies, objects, relationships, causes, and consequences remain coherent as the draft changes.

The safest approach is not to reread with the vague instruction to “look for mistakes.” Divide continuity into systems, inspect each system with evidence, and turn every finding into a specific editorial decision.

## Understand the kinds of continuity you are checking

Writers often use “continuity error” to mean a visible factual contradiction: blue eyes become brown, Tuesday becomes Friday, or a broken window repairs itself. Those errors matter, but they are only one category.

Temporal continuity covers dates, elapsed time, travel, season, age, pregnancy, healing, sleep, and parallel scenes. Knowledge continuity covers who knows what, how they learned it, and what they falsely believe. Physical continuity covers injuries, clothing, locations, entrances, exits, props, and spatial possibility. Emotional continuity covers whether a reaction follows convincingly from the relationship’s previous state. Causal continuity covers whether an event has sufficient setup and whether its consequences remain present.

World continuity applies the same test to invented systems: magic, technology, law, religion, politics, geography, and social custom. A rule does not become inconsistent merely because it has an exception. It becomes inconsistent when the exception lacks a visible cost, condition, or explanation and therefore feels like convenience.

Separating these systems prevents an enormous, unfocused pass. You can check time without simultaneously judging dialogue. You can inspect knowledge without becoming distracted by prose rhythm.

## Create a continuity ledger while the draft is moving

A ledger is a compact record of facts that create future obligations. It should not contain every noun in the manuscript. Record facts that could contradict a later scene, alter a character’s choices, or establish an expectation the reader will carry.

Useful ledger entries include:

- the scene and chapter where the fact is established;
- the exact claim or event in plain language;
- characters who know or are affected by it;
- later scenes that depend on it;
- whether the fact is stable, unresolved, disputed, or intentionally ambiguous;
- the latest revision in which it was verified.

Suppose Chapter 4 says the city gates close at sunset. Chapter 19 requires the protagonist to ride through them at midnight. The ledger does not automatically call this an error. It exposes a question. Was the rule changed? Is there a bribe, emergency protocol, hidden entrance, or authority that permits an exception? If the manuscript provides one, continuity is intact. If the scene simply forgets the rule, you have found a repairable break.

<Callout title="Continuity is not rigidity">

Characters can lie, memories can fail, rules can be broken, and plans can change. The manuscript must make the difference between contradiction and intentional instability legible.

</Callout>

## Anchor the timeline before judging it

Many timeline problems remain invisible because the draft uses soft phrases such as “later,” “the next morning,” or “after several days.” Begin with hard anchors: named dates, day counts, holidays, moon phases that matter to the plot, travel departures, school terms, election deadlines, medication schedules, and other events the reader can measure.

Then connect soft scenes to the nearest hard anchor. If a chapter contains no date, you may still know it occurs the morning after the festival and two days before the hearing. That relationship is enough to place it.

For multiple points of view, build parallel lanes. Record when each scene begins, how long it plausibly lasts, where the character is, and what messages could have reached them. This catches a common problem: two separately convincing sequences overlap in a way that makes shared characters or objects appear in two places at once.

Travel deserves explicit attention. Do not ask only whether a journey is possible. Ask what the story has already taught readers about distance, transport, weather, security, exhaustion, and preparation. A three-hour trip can become implausible if an earlier chapter spent pages establishing a flooded road.

Healing and fatigue are timelines too. A sprained ankle, concussion, sleepless night, or magical cost should shape later physical choices until the manuscript shows recovery.

## Audit character knowledge scene by scene

For every major revelation, create a knowledge chain. Who discovers the fact first? Who witnesses the discovery? Who is told, by whom, in which scene? Who remains excluded? Who receives an incomplete or false version?

This exercise catches accidental omniscience. It also reveals missed drama. If the author has allowed every character to behave as though they share the same information, the story may have skipped opportunities for misunderstanding, leverage, or emotional consequence.

Use a simple distinction between fact, belief, and suspicion. A character can possess the fact that a warehouse burned, believe the fire was accidental, and suspect a witness is lying. Later dialogue must reflect all three layers. Revision often changes one without updating the others.

When a scene moves, recheck every reaction downstream. If the confession now occurs after the council vote, earlier voters cannot use it as motivation. If a letter is intercepted rather than delivered, the intended recipient’s next choice requires a new cause.

## Follow objects and bodies through physical space

Props become continuity risks when they transfer ownership, enable action, or symbolize a promise. Track keys, weapons, letters, phones, medicine, vehicles, heirlooms, evidence, and any object a later scene needs. Note where it was last seen and who could reasonably possess it.

Spatial checks should read like stage directions. Who enters? Which door do they use? What can each person see? What blocks sound? Where is the object before and after the scene? You do not need to choreograph every cup on a table, but you do need to understand the physical logic behind consequential actions.

Bodies carry state between scenes. Record injuries, blood, dirt, wet clothing, hunger, intoxication, transformed appearance, and other conditions that should not reset at a chapter break. A character who limps in one scene and sprints in the next may be healed, masking pain, or inconsistent. The surrounding evidence decides which.

One efficient method is a “last known state” note at the end of high-action chapters. List location, companions, injuries, important objects, immediate knowledge, and unresolved danger. Use it as the opening check for the next appearance.

## Check causes and consequences, not only facts

Some of the most damaging continuity errors contain no contradictory fact. They occur when a major event fails to change the story. A public betrayal should alter trust. A death should change logistics, authority, grief, or risk. A revealed identity should affect how earlier behaviour is interpreted.

For each turning point, ask three questions:

1. What made this event possible?
2. Who now knows or believes something different?
3. What remains changed after the scene ends?

If the first answer is weak, the event may lack setup. If the second is unclear, reactions may become inconsistent. If the third disappears, the event may feel decorative no matter how dramatic the prose was.

This is also how you distinguish a plot hole from a surprise. A surprise withholds an answer while preserving a discoverable chain of cause. A plot hole has no adequate chain once the reader examines it.

## Recheck the seams created by revision

Continuity errors cluster around structural edits. Mark the seams whenever you delete a scene, merge chapters, change point of view, move a revelation, compress time, replace a location, or alter a character’s motive. Then inspect several scenes on either side.

A deleted scene may have carried more than plot. It may have explained why clothing changed, transferred an object, healed a quarrel, or taught a character a name. When you remove it, search for downstream references to every meaningful event it contained.

After moving a scene, check both chronology and emotional temperature. A confrontation that worked after three smaller provocations may feel abrupt when placed earlier. A tender exchange moved later may ignore a betrayal that now precedes it.

Keep a short revision log: what changed, why, and which continuity systems might be affected. This creates a targeted verification list instead of relying on memory at the end of a long rewrite.

<PullQuote>

The highest-risk continuity error is often not inside the scene you changed. It is in the scene that still assumes the old version happened.

</PullQuote>

## Turn every finding into an editorial decision

A continuity report is useful only when it leads to an edit. For each finding, choose one of five actions: keep, clarify, move, rewrite, or remove.

Keep the material when the apparent contradiction is intentional and sufficiently legible. Clarify when the logic is present but readers lack one connecting detail. Move when the information exists in the wrong order. Rewrite when the event’s cause or consequence has fundamentally changed. Remove when the detail creates an obligation the story no longer needs.

Prefer the lightest edit that restores trust. If one sentence can establish that the southern gate remains open during emergencies, you may not need a page explaining municipal policy. If a character’s emotional reversal lacks cause, however, a sentence of explanation may be weaker than a new beat of action.

Record the decision beside the finding and verify the affected scenes after editing. “Fixed” should mean the manuscript now supports one coherent reading, not merely that the original flag has disappeared.

## Use tools without outsourcing judgment

Search, spreadsheets, timeline boards, and manuscript-analysis tools can all reduce retrieval work. LoreVia can surface characters, chapter events, relationships, timeline signals, open threads, and grounded passages from the draft. That makes it easier to ask where a claim was established or which scenes depend on it.

No tool should be treated as the final authority on intentional ambiguity, unreliable narration, or emotional truth. Automated findings are invitations to inspect. Check the cited scene, decide whether the tension is deliberate, and make the smallest editorial change that serves the book.

The standard is not perfect agreement between a database and every sentence. The standard is reader trust. A reader should feel that the story remembers its own rules even when characters do not.

## Run a focused final continuity pass

Before sharing the manuscript, work through a repeatable sequence:

1. Map hard timeline anchors and verify elapsed time, travel, healing, and overlaps.
2. Trace major revelations through each character’s knowledge and beliefs.
3. Check consequential objects from introduction to final use.
4. Review injuries, appearance changes, entrances, exits, and last known locations.
5. Inspect relationship turns and the reactions that follow them.
6. List setups, promises, mysteries, and rules; label each paid off, deferred, or removed.
7. Revisit every structural revision seam and confirm the new version propagates forward.

Do not perform all seven passes at once. A narrow lens makes contradictions easier to see and decisions easier to record.

Continuity control is not about making fiction mechanically tidy. It protects the conditions under which surprise, emotion, and invention can work. When readers trust that the story remembers, they stop checking the scaffolding and give their attention to what the characters risk on it.
