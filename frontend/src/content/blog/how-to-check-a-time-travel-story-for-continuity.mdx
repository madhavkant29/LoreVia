Time travel does not need to be physically explained to be narratively coherent. It does need to tell readers what kind of causality they are watching.

Can the past change? Does a change replace one history, create a branch, or fulfil what always happened? Who remembers the previous version? Can an object meet its earlier self? What prevents a traveller from repeating the journey until every choice succeeds?

In Leena’s novel, she returns three times to a railway station on the night her sister disappears. On the first visit, she hides a key. On the second, she finds the key already hidden. On the third, she prevents her younger self from entering the station—but still remembers the original disappearance. The scenes are individually clear. Their shared model is not.

A continuity audit starts by choosing causal rules, then follows events, travellers, information, objects, and memory through every version.

## State the model in plain language

You do not need to explain the machine. You need an author-facing statement of what changes do.

Common structures include:

- **fixed history:** travel always formed part of the one existing past;
- **mutable history:** changing the past rewrites the traveller’s present;
- **branching history:** intervention creates or enters another timeline;
- **limited correction:** some events can shift while others resist or recur;
- **subjective travel:** the journey changes memory, perception, or identity rather than public history.

Hybrids are possible, but name the conditions that switch behaviour. If personal choices branch while major events remain fixed, define what counts as major and how characters discover that limit.

Write three example consequences under your rule before revising the plot. What happens if Leena steals her own ticket, saves a stranger, or kills an ancestor? Edge cases expose vagueness.

## Give every event a stable identity

Assign identifiers to important events independent of chapter and version. The station fire might be `E-STATION-FIRE`. Then record its state in each timeline or iteration: occurs unchanged, occurs differently, prevented, displaced, or unknown.

This avoids treating similar events as automatically the same. A fire at 22:10 started by Leena may replace a fire at 22:30 started by the porter. The location and outcome overlap, but cause, witnesses, and evidence differ.

For each version, record cause, time, participants, result, and which later events depend on it. If the event changes, follow those dependencies forward.

Fixed-history stories need the same record. It shows whether a traveller’s intervention closes the loop or introduces a contradiction.

## Build separate lines for world and traveller

The world moves chronologically; the traveller experiences a different order. Keep both.

Leena’s personal sequence may be:

1. Live through the original disappearance in 2035.
2. Travel to the station in 2012 and hide the key.
3. Return to 2035 and find a changed newspaper.
4. Travel again to 2012, arriving twenty minutes earlier.

The station’s 2012 sequence must include both versions of Leena in local order. This dual record catches conversations in which an older traveller refers to an experience the younger traveller has not had.

Add age, injuries, carried knowledge, and emotional state to the traveller line. Calendar age and experienced age may diverge.

## Track memory as its own rule

Mutable timelines often become inconsistent through memory. Decide who remembers overwritten history, when memories update, whether new memories are acquired, and what physical or emotional traces remain.

If Leena remembers the original disappearance after preventing it, why? Travel may insulate memory, the rewrite may arrive gradually, or she may hold both versions. Each choice creates effects.

Record memory per event and version. “Leena remembers Timeline A but has no lived memory of Timeline B” differs from “Leena acquires both and cannot distinguish them.” Let speech, grief, skill, and relationships reflect the state.

Other people may notice contradiction through photographs, scars, records, or emotional residue. If only travellers remember, institutions should respond accordingly to claims nobody can verify.

## Follow objects through custody and duplication

Objects can loop, age, duplicate, vanish, or become their own origin. Give important objects a custody ledger with version, holder, physical age, and causal origin.

The station key is forged in 2008, hidden by older Leena in 2012, found by younger Leena later that night, kept until 2035, and carried back to 2012. At one interval, two physically different instances may coexist unless the carried key is the same object completing a loop.

Decide what happens if instances touch or one is destroyed. The reader need not receive a lecture, but the climax must not improvise.

Bootstrap objects or information with no external origin can be intentional. Mark the loop and ask what thematic or practical cost makes it more than a shortcut.

## Audit information for causal origin

A warning passed from future to past can create itself. Leena knows the 22:10 train will derail because her sister told her; the sister knows because Leena warned her years earlier. This closed loop may fit the model. The information has circulation but no first discovery.

List every plot-critical fact, who learns it, from which version, and whether it has an origin outside the loop. If originlessness is allowed, apply it consistently and decide why characters cannot manufacture unlimited perfect knowledge.

Beware author knowledge. A traveller may know the original timeline but not the consequences of changes they have not yet observed. Prediction should become less reliable as divergence grows.

## Define travel access, limits, and cost

Without limits, readers will ask why characters do not try again. State who can travel, destination precision, frequency, capacity, required resources, and consequences.

Cost can include bodily harm, lost lifespan, memory erosion, increasing divergence, inability to revisit the same moment, attention from an authority, or damage to the destination. It must affect choices.

Leena can revisit the station only by surrendering a memory connected to her sister. The first trip costs a birthday, the second a shared song, the third the sound of her voice. Repetition remains possible but changes what saving her means.

Avoid a limit that vanishes at the climax. If emotion expands range, establish that relationship earlier and carry its cost.

<Callout title="The retry question">

After every failure and partial success, ask why the traveller does not simply attempt the same intervention again. Put the actual answer into character, rule, or consequence.

</Callout>

## Check encounters with the self

If versions can coexist, map local positions and knowledge. Which Leena is present, what age is she, whom does she recognize, and what would contact change?

Distinguish visual identity from legal and relational identity. A younger self may not believe the traveller. A family member may notice a habit. Cameras and records may capture duplicates even if nobody understands them.

If contact is forbidden or dangerous, define whether the rule is physical, institutional, or merely believed. A feared paradox that proves false is a change to world knowledge and should affect later strategy.

Use consistent labels in notes—Leena-35A, Leena-35B—not in polished prose. The reader can follow through context if the author’s map is exact.

## Revise changes through dependency chains

When an intervention changes an event, list every dependent condition: people alive, objects available, messages sent, institutions formed, relationships begun, and motives created.

Preventing the station entry may save Leena’s sister but also prevent her meeting the porter who later builds the travel device. The next version cannot keep the rescue and the device without another cause.

This is where mutable stories often preserve emotionally convenient pieces of both histories. Decide what the rule preserves and why. Branching models can retain both, but characters need a route between branches if consequences cross.

Revise from the earliest changed cause forward, one version at a time.

## Let characters test the model

Characters should not instantly know the author’s rules. Give them hypotheses, experiments, failures, and competing interpretations. Their understanding can develop while observed behaviour stays consistent.

Leena hides a newspaper in the station wall to test whether changes persist. She asks her future self a question no record contains. She discovers that objects update immediately while her memories do not. These tests reveal rules through action.

Avoid making every experiment clean. Interference, incomplete observation, and emotional bias can complicate inference without changing the underlying model.

## Track records, photographs, and digital evidence

Mutable history becomes visible through records. Decide whether documents update, duplicate, disappear, or preserve prior versions. Apply the rule consistently to photographs, databases, scars, messages, recordings, and other evidence.

If Leena returns to 2035 after changing the station, the newspaper may now report a different disappearance. Does the paper transform in her hand, or does she arrive holding an artefact from the origin timeline? The answer depends on whether carried matter is insulated like her memory.

Create an evidence table with item, origin version, current version, observer, update rule, and plot use. This prevents one photograph from preserving erased history while another updates solely because the reveal needs it.

Institutions should react to anomalies. A timestamped video of two Leenas may create investigation even when nobody believes time travel. Altered school records can change legal identity. Digital systems may propagate updates differently from physical objects only if your model establishes that distinction.

Records also affect emotion. Leena may remember a sister who no longer remembers their childhood, while photographs show a life Leena never lived. Let evidence complicate belonging rather than serving only as proof of mechanics.

When records are unreliable by rule, give characters other ways to test change: shared codes, physical caches, bodily marks, or independent witnesses. The methods should have limits and leave traces of their own.

## Audit the ending in every surviving version

Write the final state of each timeline or history: who exists, what they remember, which objects remain, which events occurred, and what access to travel survives.

If the story ends in a branch, consider the original world. Has Leena left it behind? Does that cost matter? If history is overwritten, what happens to relationships formed only in the erased version? If the loop closes, which choices were free and what meaning does the character assign them?

The ending need not answer every metaphysical question. It must honour consequences the model has made emotionally active.

Before the final pass, search dialogue for loose words such as “before,” “again,” “first time,” “already,” and “remember.” Their reference point may differ for the traveller, the local character, and the reader. Replace ambiguity only when it obscures the intended version; elsewhere, let it create controlled double meaning.

Leena calling the station visit “our first meeting” may be true for her sister and false for Leena. The manuscript should know whether the line is a mistake, concealment, or grief.

In Leena’s revised novel, history branches. Each station visit creates a new line, travellers retain memories from their origin, and carried objects can duplicate. The final choice is not to perfect one past but to decide which sister she can help and which world she must leave. The rules make the cost legible.

Time travel becomes moving when continuity is more than bookkeeping. A coherent model lets impossible events produce reliable consequences, so characters can make choices that remain meaningful even when history does not remain still.
