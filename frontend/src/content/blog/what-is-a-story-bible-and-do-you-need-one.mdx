A story bible is the record of what your manuscript has made true. It holds the names, relationships, locations, rules, events, objects, promises, and decisions that future pages must remember. Its job is not to prove that you planned thoroughly. Its job is to help you retrieve the right fact before you contradict it.

That distinction matters because story bibles have a reputation for becoming elaborate side projects. An author creates beautiful character questionnaires, detailed maps, family trees, calendars, and pages of history, then stops updating everything when the actual drafting becomes difficult. The bible remains polished and the manuscript moves on without it.

A useful story bible is smaller, less decorative, and more alive. It privileges facts that create obligations. It records where those facts appear. It changes when the book changes. And it remains close enough to the writing process that checking it is easier than trusting memory.

## What a story bible is for

The bible gives you a shared source of truth across drafting, revision, collaboration, and future books. When you wonder whether the apothecary has appeared before, you should be able to find the answer without searching every chapter. When an editor asks why the northern road is closed, the record should point to the scene where the flood was established. When book two returns to a minor promise from book one, you should know the original wording and who heard it.

The record is especially valuable when the story grows in one of four directions: a large cast, a long timeline, a rule-heavy setting, or a series. It also helps smaller books when their emotional logic depends on subtle relationship changes or knowledge moving unevenly between characters.

The bible should answer editorial questions, not merely collect information. “What colour is the inn?” may matter once. “Who owns the inn, who is allowed upstairs, and which secret was hidden there?” is more likely to influence the plot.

A story bible is a maintained index of canon and unresolved obligations, linked to the manuscript evidence that established them.

</Callout>

## The core sections worth keeping

Most novels need six connected areas. You can keep them in one document or separate views, but the categories help you avoid building a bible that is only a cast list.

**Characters** need canonical names, aliases, roles, first appearances, stable traits, knowledge, goals, wounds, possessions, and unresolved promises. Store changing information as a timeline rather than overwriting the old state.

**Relationships** need their own entries. Record the nature of the bond, power balance, current tension, scenes that changed it, misunderstandings still active, and later payoffs that depend on earlier movement.

**Places** need geography only to the degree that geography controls action. Include travel relationships, access rules, sensory identity, ownership, hazards, and scenes that established important features.

**Timeline** entries need hard anchors, elapsed time, travel, healing, parallel events, and the order in which information arrives. A list of chapter numbers is not a timeline unless it clarifies when events occur relative to one another.

**World rules** include magic, technology, law, religion, social custom, politics, economics, and any repeated condition that shapes possibility. Each rule should state its limits, cost, known exceptions, and the scene that demonstrated it.

**Threads and objects** include promises, mysteries, debts, prophecies, evidence, heirlooms, keys, weapons, and any setup that creates a reader expectation. Mark each open, paid off, deliberately deferred, or removed.

These areas should link. A world rule affects characters. A relationship turn happens at a place. An object moves through a timeline. Separate categories improve retrieval, but the story remains a network.

## Record canon, intention, and uncertainty separately

One reason bibles become unreliable is that they mix three kinds of statement. Canon is visible in the manuscript. Intention is what the author plans to establish later. Uncertainty is a question still under development.

Consider these notes:

- “The river freezes every winter.”
- “The river should freeze before the siege.”
- “Would the river freeze this far south?”

Only the first is canon, and even that claim needs a source scene. The second is a plan. The third is an open design question. If all three appear as equivalent facts, future-you may treat an idea as established history.

Use clear statuses such as confirmed, planned, disputed, intentionally ambiguous, and retired. “Disputed” is useful when characters or sources inside the story disagree. “Retired” preserves the history of a fact that appeared in an older draft but should no longer guide new writing.

This separation is essential for mystery and unreliable narration. The bible can record what happened, what each character believes happened, and what the reader currently has reason to believe without flattening them into one answer.

## Tie important entries to manuscript evidence

Every high-risk fact should have a chapter or scene reference. The reference makes the record auditable. It also prevents the bible from quietly becoming more authoritative than the book.

Suppose the location page says the archive has only one entrance. Later, a chase scene uses a service stair. Is that a contradiction? The answer depends on the original passage. Perhaps “only one public entrance” was the actual wording. Reopening the scene resolves the question faster than debating the summary.

Evidence can be concise: “Ch. 8 — keeper says the east door has been bricked up” or “Scene 24 — Nia gives the brass key to Oran.” Quote only when exact wording matters, as with oaths, riddles, laws, or promises that later return.

Chapter evidence also improves collaboration. Editors and co-authors can verify a claim without relying on the person who entered it. If the reference is obsolete after a structural edit, the stale link becomes a visible maintenance task.

## Build the bible from the draft, not around it

Planning-first authors may create a small bible before drafting. Discovery writers may begin after several chapters. Both approaches work if the record follows the manuscript rather than attempting to predict all of it.

Start with material that already creates constraints. Add named characters, recurring places, explicit rules, hard timeline anchors, and open promises. Leave empty categories empty. Do not invent three centuries of history because the template includes a history field.

At the end of each drafting session or chapter, capture only what changed. Which new person appeared? What fact became canon? Who learned something? What object moved? Which relationship turned? Which promise opened or closed? This five-minute habit keeps the record alive without turning drafting into administration.

During revision, update the bible after structural changes. A moved revelation affects knowledge entries. A deleted journey affects the timeline. A merged character affects relationships, aliases, and every scene reference. The bible is most valuable precisely when the draft is least stable.

## Decide when you actually need one

You probably need a story bible when you repeatedly search the manuscript for the same facts, when readers find contradictions you thought you had tracked, or when revision changes ripple through multiple systems. A series, shared universe, game narrative, or collaborative project should establish one early because memory is distributed across people and time.

You may not need a formal bible for a short, linear story with a small cast and few recurring constraints. A two-page continuity sheet might be enough. Do not adopt the largest system simply because professional productions use one.

Use friction as the test. If you lose time finding facts, the record is too small or poorly indexed. If you lose time maintaining facts that never influence writing, it is too large. The right bible reduces total effort across the life of the project.

Another test is absence. If you can leave the draft for three months and return without rereading everything to recover character state, open threads, and world rules, the bible is doing its job.

## Choose a format that supports retrieval

A single document works for a compact novel. Use clear headings and links. A spreadsheet works for repeatable fields such as character appearances, timeline anchors, or objects moving between scenes. A database or dedicated story tool works when entries need many relationships and filters. Cards can help during active planning but become harder to search as the project grows.

Whatever the format, insist on:

- fast search;
- links between related records;
- chapter or scene references;
- visible status for uncertain and retired facts;
- a revision date or version note;
- an export path that does not trap your material.

Templates are useful only if they remain editable. Remove fields you never consult. Add fields for the actual pressures of your genre: alibis in a mystery, treaty obligations in political fantasy, symptom progression in medical drama, or public/private relationship states in romance.

## Let software prepare the record without surrendering it

Automatic extraction can reduce the blank-page work of a story bible. LoreVia can identify chapter structure, characters, aliases, relationships, timeline signals, and recurring manuscript details, then connect those findings back to passages. This is particularly helpful for discovery writers who finished a draft without maintaining reference material.

Extraction is a proposal. Names may be duplicated, titles may look like separate people, a flashback may confuse chronological order, and a metaphor may resemble a factual statement. Review the record, merge duplicates, correct labels, and mark uncertainty. An imperfect first pass is useful when it is easy to verify; an unreviewed one is merely confident.

The author remains responsible for intentional ambiguity. A system can surface that two scenes describe the same event differently. Only you can decide whether the difference is a mistake, a lie, a memory failure, or the point of the book.

## Prevent the bible from becoming a museum

The common failure is not that authors never build a bible. It is that they stop maintaining it. The record freezes while the manuscript continues changing, then becomes dangerous because old information still looks official.

Create a maintenance rule tied to work you already do. Update after a chapter is approved, after a structural revision sprint, before sending a new draft, and before planning the next book. Keep a small “changed this revision” queue so you do not have to edit every connected entry in the middle of a creative decision.

Audit the highest-risk areas first: names, ages, knowledge, travel, injuries, world rules, relationship status, and open promises. Archive retired facts rather than deleting all history, especially in a series where an earlier edition or collaborator may still reference them.

The best story bible is not the most complete. It is the one you trust enough to consult and light enough to keep current.

## A practical setup for your next session

Begin with six pages or views: characters, relationships, places, timeline, rules, and open threads. Add only confirmed facts from the existing draft. Give every consequential entry a source. Mark plans and questions visibly. Then choose one maintenance moment, such as the end of each chapter or revision day.

After a week, notice what you searched for that the bible could not answer. Add that field or link. Notice what you recorded but never used. Remove it. Let the system evolve around the demands of the manuscript.

A story bible cannot make a story coherent on its own. It can make incoherence visible sooner, preserve decisions you have already earned, and release your attention from remembering logistics. That leaves more room for the work a reference document cannot do: finding the choice, image, or line that makes the story feel alive.
