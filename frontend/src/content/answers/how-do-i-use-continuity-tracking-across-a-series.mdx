Track series continuity by separating facts that remain stable from states that change. Record the book and chapter where canon is established, keep chronological histories for location, knowledge, allegiance, injuries, possessions, and relationships, and document deliberate retcons instead of silently overwriting earlier evidence.

## Create a series-level canon layer

Some facts should be shared across every volume: geography, calendar rules, institutional history, magic limits, family structures, naming conventions, and major past events. Store each fact with a source and status.

Useful statuses include confirmed on page, author-only plan, disputed in-world claim, superseded by deliberate retcon, and unresolved. This prevents a villain’s propaganda in book one from becoming accidental objective truth in book three.

Keep author plans visually separate from published canon. Once a volume is released, its visible facts create obligations even if your private outline once said something else.

## Track state over time

“Ari lives in Bellwater” cannot be a permanent profile field if Ari moves. Use a history:

- Book one, chapters 1–14: Bellwater.
- Book one, chapter 15: departs for the capital.
- Book two, chapters 1–8: capital barracks.
- Book two, chapter 9: location concealed from viewpoint cast.

Apply the same method to titles, injuries, alliances, knowledge, object ownership, and relationship states. The question for a new scene becomes “What is true at this point?” rather than “What does the profile say?”

<Callout title="Canon needs a timestamp">

Stable identity facts can live on a profile. Anything that changes through story time belongs on a history.

</Callout>

## Preserve evidence across volumes

Attach book, chapter, and a short context note to consequential facts. Page numbers vary by edition, so they should not be the only locator. If the exact wording matters—a vow, prophecy, legal clause, or magic rule—preserve a brief excerpt only in your private working record and confirm it against the published text.

When sources conflict, do not immediately pick one. Record both and identify the narrator or speaker. The conflict may be intentional mystery.

## Run a pre-draft check

Before drafting a sequel, review the state at the previous ending:

- Where is each continuing character?
- What do they know and believe?
- Which promises and debts remain active?
- What injuries, resources, titles, and relationships changed?
- Which rules were tested or given exceptions?
- Which public events would have continuing consequences?

Use that snapshot as the new book’s starting condition. It will catch ideas that belong to an earlier outline but no longer fit canon.

## Run a scene-level check during revision

For each scene in the new volume, verify the facts it consumes. Suppose book three has Ren cross a bridge that collapsed in book one. Several solutions may be valid: the bridge was rebuilt, a temporary crossing exists, the “bridge” is a local name for the route, or the draft contains an error. Add the necessary evidence instead of quietly ignoring the history.

Check callbacks for emotional continuity too. A reunion should reflect the last actual interaction, not the warmer relationship you originally planned.

## Handle retcons transparently

Sometimes the new story needs a change. Record the original canon, the revised interpretation, the volume that introduces it, and the explanation readers receive. Prefer expansion or revealed misunderstanding when it serves the story, but do not force an elaborate justification for every small inconsistency.

Never delete the old entry. You need it to understand what returning readers may remember and which scenes could require explanation.

## Common mistakes

Do not duplicate separate bibles for each book without a shared canon layer. They will drift.

Do not store changing states as timeless facts.

Do not let author-only plans leak into summaries of what readers know.

Do not cite only page numbers or memory.

Do not update the bible at the end of an entire draft. Record consequential decisions while they are still clear.

Do not check facts alone. Character knowledge and relationship continuity often cause the more damaging series errors.

## A sustainable series check

At each milestone, capture the ending state of the volume, update shared canon, record unresolved questions, and archive the source version. Before the next book, review that snapshot; during revision, compare every consequential callback with its evidence.

## Review at each release boundary

Before a volume is final, compare its opening state with the prior book’s ending and capture its own ending state for the next project. Review every returning character, recurring place, active rule, unresolved promise, and consequential object. Record intentional changes with their explanation and first on-page evidence.

After publication, treat visible facts as history. Future planning may extend or reinterpret them, but it should not silently replace them. This release-boundary habit turns continuity from a memory test into a repeatable editorial check.

Archive that snapshot with the published volume.

Give every collaborator access to the same clearly labelled canonical record.
