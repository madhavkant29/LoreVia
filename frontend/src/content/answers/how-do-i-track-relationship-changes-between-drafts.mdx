Track relationship changes between drafts by comparing turning points, not files line by line. Build a compact ledger for the old and new versions. For every consequential interaction, record the starting relationship state, the decision or revelation, its cost, and the resulting state. Then match scenes by function even when chapter numbers and wording have changed.

## Freeze the comparison versions

Name both manuscripts clearly. Keep the earlier draft unchanged and note the dates, total chapters, and major structural changes. If you compare against a moving file, the record will become unreliable.

Choose which relationship you are examining and why. You may be testing whether a reconciliation is earned, whether an antagonist’s leverage remains clear, or whether a mentor’s betrayal still affects the climax. A bounded question determines which scenes matter.

## Build a ledger for each draft

For every meaningful interaction, record:

- a brief scene description;
- viewpoint and location;
- the relationship state entering the scene;
- what each person wants from the other;
- new information or changed power;
- the decision, refusal, or action that creates the turn;
- the cost;
- the state leaving the scene.

Use state language that carries tension: guarded dependence, public alliance and private doubt, unequal obligation, open hostility with residual affection. “Friends” is usually too broad.

<Callout title="Match by dramatic function">

“First voluntary act of trust” can be compared across drafts even if it moved from chapter six to chapter ten and now occurs in a different location.

</Callout>

## Compare the turning points

Place the ledgers side by side and mark each old beat as retained, transformed, moved, merged, split, or removed. Add new beats separately. Ask what the revision changed in cause, cost, and consequence.

Suppose draft one has Ada reveal her secret after Ben rescues her. In draft two, she reveals it before the rescue so he can choose whether to help. The plot information is similar, but the relationship function changes. The second version gives Ada more agency and makes Ben’s action a test of trust. Your comparison should capture that difference.

## Look for missing transitions

Structural revision often preserves the major conflict and reconciliation while deleting the quiet beat that connected them. Read only the resulting relationship states in order. If distrust becomes intimacy without an observable decision or new evidence, a transition may be missing.

Also look for duplicate turns. Two apology scenes may both move hostility to wary cooperation. Keep both only if the second changes the terms, raises the cost, or reveals a new layer.

Track who initiates each change. A revision can accidentally make one person passive by transferring every decisive action to the other.

## Check knowledge and chronology

A scene may still exist but no longer work because its preconditions moved. Confirm what each character knows, what promises are active, and how much time has passed. If Ben now learns Ada’s secret earlier, later suspicion cannot proceed unchanged.

Compare event order separately from chapter order when the novel uses flashbacks or multiple viewpoints. The reader’s understanding of a relationship can change before the characters’ relationship does.

## Turn differences into choices

Not every change requires repair. Classify differences:

- **intentional improvement:** supports the new structure;
- **neutral consequence:** wording or placement changed without altering function;
- **continuity risk:** later behaviour assumes a removed state;
- **arc gap:** an essential turn or cost disappeared;
- **new opportunity:** the revision creates stronger contrast or agency.

For each risk, decide the desired relationship state and the smallest scene-level change that can earn it. Avoid restoring old material merely because it existed.

## Common mistakes

Do not compare chapter numbers as if they were stable identities. Use scene function and participants.

Do not track only dialogue. Actions, silence, gifts, withheld knowledge, public loyalties, and physical distance can carry the turn.

Do not reduce a complex bond to a single score. Trust may rise while power becomes less equal.

Do not forget secondary effects. Changing a central relationship can alter alliances around it.

Do not keep notes without draft labels. A correct observation about draft three can become a false warning in draft four.

## The revision payoff

At the end, you should be able to describe what the new draft changed in the relationship and point to the scenes that earn each major state. The ledger is not a demand to preserve the old book. It is a way to notice what the new one has gained or accidentally lost.

## A final comparison

Summarize each draft’s relationship in three sentences: the starting bargain, the turn with the greatest cost, and the state at the ending. If the new summary describes the intended change and every sentence has a scene behind it, the revision has retained a coherent relational arc.
