A continuity checker helps an author compare facts that are easy to forget because they appear far apart. It may connect a character’s eye colour in chapter three with a description in chapter twenty, or compare a departure time with an arrival that seems impossible. The checker surfaces a candidate conflict. The author decides whether the difference is an error, an intentional change, or an ambiguity worth clarifying.

## What it checks

Useful checks tend to fall into several groups:

- **Identity:** names, aliases, ages, family ties, titles, appearance, and possessions.
- **Time:** dates, sequence, duration, deadlines, seasons, travel, and recovery.
- **Place:** distances, entrances, room layouts, borders, and who can reach a location.
- **Knowledge:** what each character knows, when they learned it, and whether they could share it.
- **State:** injuries, clothing, weather, object ownership, relationships, and world rules.
- **Promises:** mysteries, plans, warnings, debts, and planted details that later scenes should address.

This is broader than proofreading. A proofreader can correct grammar and local inconsistency, but a story fact may require evidence from hundreds of pages apart.

## How a checker finds risks

Some tools rely on direct matching. They collect mentions of a name, date, place, or object and make them easier to compare. Others extract structured facts or use language models to answer questions about passages. Both approaches can save retrieval time, but both can misread context.

For example, “Jon had never crossed the sea” and “Jon remembered the harbour at Calais” deserve inspection. They may contradict each other. They may also reflect a lie, a dream, adoption of another identity, or a harbour seen from the same shore. The checker cannot settle that distinction without the author’s intended truth.

<Callout title="Treat flags as editorial questions">

The safest output is: “These two passages may conflict; inspect chapters four and nineteen.” It is not: “The manuscript is wrong.”

</Callout>

## A reliable continuity pass

1. Choose one domain, such as time or character knowledge.
2. Review high-consequence facts before cosmetic ones.
3. Open every cited passage and read its scene context.
4. Label each finding confirmed, intentional, unclear, or false positive.
5. Decide which established fact is canon.
6. Search for every downstream mention before editing.
7. Recheck the revised scenes together.

Imagine a heist novel in which Sela breaks her right wrist. Four chapters later she climbs a drainpipe, and the next morning another character asks about her injured left hand. These are two different issues: physical feasibility and a side mismatch. You might revise the climb, clarify that she uses a brace and help, and standardize the injured side. Fixing only the word “left” would leave the more serious problem untouched.

## What a checker cannot decide

Continuity is not the same as realism. A magical door may move every night if the book establishes that rule. A narrator may contradict himself because the narrative is testing his reliability. A scene may skip an explanation because withholding it improves suspense.

The checker also cannot rank every problem by emotional importance. A slightly changed tablecloth may not matter. A character forgiving someone before learning the truth may break the climax. Human reading determines consequence.

## Common mistakes

Do not run one enormous pass and treat every alert equally. Fatigue makes authors dismiss good findings. Work by domain and stop when your judgment becomes shallow.

Do not “correct” the manuscript before choosing canon. If two ages conflict, determine which supports the chronology, backstory, and later books. Then update all affected passages and the story bible.

Do not erase productive ambiguity. If readers are meant to doubt whether a memory occurred, adding a precise explanation may damage the book. Record the ambiguity as intentional so it is not repeatedly flagged.

Finally, do not trust a report generated from an old draft. Continuity changes with every scene move. Name the source manuscript and rerun focused checks after structural revision.

## The useful standard

A good continuity checker shortens the distance between suspicion and evidence. It helps you locate the right passages, compare them, and record a decision. It should make close reading more effective, not pretend to replace it.

## Evaluate the checker itself

Test a continuity checker with facts you already understand. Include an obvious match, an intentional contradiction, an alias, a disputed statement, and an event told out of order. Inspect whether the tool cites the relevant passages and expresses uncertainty instead of forcing one answer.

Also test absence. Ask for the last meaningful appearance of a subplot and confirm the surrounding gap yourself. A trustworthy checker should help you retrieve evidence without concealing its limits. If you cannot trace a consequential warning back to the manuscript, do not use it as the basis for revision.

Evidence must remain visible, specific, and reviewable.

The author’s close reading remains the final and most important check.
