Prepare a beta draft so readers can experience the story without fighting the document. Finish the structural pass you intended to test, correct distracting errors, label the version, and provide a concise brief. Your goal is not to make the manuscript publication-perfect. It is to ensure the feedback reflects the story problem you are trying to solve.

## Decide what this round must teach you

Beta reading works best when it answers a limited set of revision decisions. You may need to know whether the midpoint changes the reader’s understanding, whether a romance feels earned, whether the mystery can be followed, or where tension collapses.

Write the questions before recruiting readers. This reveals whether the draft is ready. If you are still deciding between two entirely different endings, test that deliberately rather than pretending the book is stable.

Avoid asking readers to assess everything. “Tell me what you think” often produces scattered proofreading and personal preference. Focused questions produce comparable observations.

## Stabilize the manuscript

Complete the full draft, unless the readers explicitly agreed to test an opening or serial section. Resolve placeholder chapters, duplicated scenes, missing names, and notes such as “fight goes here.” These gaps force readers to imagine material that later readers will actually encounter.

Run a practical continuity pass on dates, names, viewpoint labels, chapter order, and major relationship states. Correct typos that interrupt comprehension. You do not need a final copyedit, but readers should not spend their attention solving preventable confusion.

Give the file a version name such as `River_Glass_Beta_2026-08-09`. Keep an unchanged master so every comment can be traced to the same text.

## Format for sustained reading

Use a legible typeface, comfortable size and spacing, clear chapter breaks, page numbers where the format supports them, and consistent scene separators. Include the title and author name. Remove tracked changes and private editorial comments.

Ask each reader which format they can actually use: DOCX for comments, PDF for stable pages, EPUB for an e-reader, or a private reading platform. Convenience increases completion.

## Write a brief that does not lead the reader

The brief should include:

- a two- or three-sentence premise;
- the draft stage and what has already been revised;
- the deadline and expected time commitment;
- instructions for comments or a response form;
- three to seven priority questions;
- content notes readers need to consent meaningfully;
- a request not to share the manuscript.

Do not explain why a character “really” behaves a certain way. If that intention must be supplied outside the book, the reader cannot test whether the pages communicate it.

“Where did your trust in Ada change?” is usually more revealing than “Should I make Ada confess in chapter twelve?”

## Choose readers for the question

Recruit people who read the genre and can complete the draft on schedule. Add sensitivity or subject-matter readers when the work requires their specific experience, but do not treat beta readers as substitutes for professional expertise.

Different readers can serve different roles. One may follow emotional logic closely; another may notice chronology; another may represent a key audience. Tell them which lens is especially valuable without preventing spontaneous reactions.

Avoid relying only on friends who know your intentions. Familiarity can make them fill gaps unconsciously.

## Protect independent responses

Collect initial feedback separately. A group discussion held too early can cause one confident reader to set the interpretation for everyone. Once individual responses are captured, conversation can clarify patterns.

Tell readers they can stop. A clear opt-out respects their time and produces better data than reluctant completion.

When feedback arrives, do not revise immediately or defend scenes. Thank the reader, ask neutral clarification questions, and let all responses accumulate. Track recurring effects rather than taking a vote on proposed fixes.

## Common mistakes

Do not send a live file you continue editing. Page references and comments will drift.

Do not bury the deadline in a long email. Make the deliverable, date, and response method obvious.

Do not ask readers to diagnose craft terminology. They can report boredom, confusion, disbelief, or emotional distance. You decide what revision may address it.

Do not include twenty questions. Readers will rush or ignore them. Prioritize the decisions that could change the next draft.

## Ready means testable

A beta manuscript is ready when it offers a continuous reading experience, represents the version you want assessed, and arrives with a respectful, focused request. The draft does not need to be perfect. It needs to be stable enough that reader responses point toward the story rather than the packaging.

## Before you send

Open the actual attachment from a test message, verify the title and version, and read the brief as if you had made no earlier agreement. The reader should know what to read, when to respond, how to respond, and how to opt out without searching another conversation.
