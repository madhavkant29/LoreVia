The aim is not to remember every detail. It is to build a retrieval system for details that become expensive when forgotten. A long manuscript will contain thousands of descriptions, objects, gestures, names, and passing facts. Most can remain on the page. Track the ones that create a future obligation.

## Choose details by risk

A detail deserves a record when at least one condition is true:

- it may contradict a later description;
- a character’s action depends on it;
- it moves between people or places;
- it changes over time;
- the prose gives it enough emphasis to create an expectation;
- finding it again would require a costly reread.

Eye colour may be worth recording for a recurring character. The colour of a stranger’s cup probably is not. A coat becomes high-risk if it conceals evidence, identifies someone, or changes owners. An injury matters while it limits action. Weather matters when travel, visibility, or mood depends on it.

This risk test keeps the ledger small enough to maintain.

## Store state, source, and consequence

For each detail, record three things:

1. **State:** what is true now.
2. **Source:** the chapter or scene where it became true.
3. **Consequence:** what later action, expectation, or continuity check depends on it.

For example: “Brass key — Nessa takes it from the caretaker in Ch. 6 — required to enter the archive in Ch. 14.” This note is more useful than “Nessa has a brass key” because it includes movement and purpose.

For changing details, preserve a short timeline instead of overwriting the old state. An injury can progress from fresh, bandaged, limiting, and healed. An object can move from owner to owner. A promise can be made, complicated, broken, and repaired.

## Use a few practical categories

You can keep one ledger with tags or separate lists. Common categories are:

- physical appearance and temporary clothing;
- injuries, illness, fatigue, hunger, and intoxication;
- objects, evidence, money, vehicles, and keys;
- weather, season, time of day, and travel conditions;
- names, titles, forms of address, and pronouns;
- knowledge, secrets, lies, and mistaken beliefs;
- promises, debts, tasks, and unresolved side threads;
- location state, damage, access, and ownership.

Use categories for retrieval, not perfection. If a wedding ring is both object and relationship evidence, one linked record is enough.

## Capture details at natural pauses

Do not stop after every sentence. At the end of a chapter or drafting session, ask:

- What new fact became stable?
- What changed hands?
- Who learned something?
- What physical state should carry forward?
- What did the scene promise to return to?

This review takes minutes and catches details while context is fresh. During revision, repeat it after scenes move, merge, or disappear. Those edits create the highest risk because later chapters may still assume the previous sequence.

## Search before adding a duplicate note

When a detail returns, search the manuscript and the ledger. Verify exact wording and aliases. The author may remember “green coat” while the page says “olive jacket,” or remember that the key was silver when only its chain was silver.

Attach the chapter reference rather than copying a long quotation. Quote exact language only when a riddle, oath, description, or clue depends on it.

If two passages conflict, do not silently choose one in the ledger. Flag the conflict, inspect which version serves the current story, and propagate the decision through affected scenes.

## Run a return check for absent characters

Small details become most vulnerable when a character disappears for many chapters. Before writing or revising the return, check their last known:

- location and companions;
- physical and emotional condition;
- possessions and clothing that matter;
- knowledge and active beliefs;
- promises, debts, and unfinished tasks;
- relationship state with people in the returning scene.

Then account for elapsed story time and any off-page change that readers need to understand.

## Let software reduce retrieval, not judgment

A spreadsheet, note system, or database can all work. Manuscript-aware tools such as LoreVia can surface characters, chapter appearances, relationships, timeline signals, and relevant passages. Review extracted details and correct them where aliases, metaphor, or nonlinear scenes confuse the first pass.

The system should make verification faster than rereading. If maintaining it takes longer than the problem it solves, remove fields and track fewer, higher-risk details.

## A five-minute continuity habit

At the end of a writing session:

1. Record new high-risk facts with chapter references.
2. Update objects, injuries, knowledge, and promises that changed.
3. Close or retire details that received a payoff.
4. Flag anything that conflicts with an earlier entry.
5. Note which later scene depends on the change.

You do not need a perfect memory or an exhaustive encyclopedia. You need a dependable answer to “Where did this become true?” Once that answer is easy to retrieve, small details stop consuming creative attention and start supporting the story quietly.

## A final memory test

Ask whether the record helps you act, not merely remember. A useful detail should reveal which chapter to check, what later scene depends on it, or why the variation is intentional. If it does none of those things, it may not deserve space in the active index.
