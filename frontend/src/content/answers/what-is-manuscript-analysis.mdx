Manuscript analysis turns a draft into a set of facts and patterns you can inspect. It asks who appears, what changes, where events happen, when knowledge moves, which rules are established, and which promises remain open. The result should help you make editorial decisions. It is not a score for whether the novel is good.

## What analysis should examine

A practical analysis begins with observable material. It can inventory characters and aliases, scenes and chapters, locations, objects, relationship changes, time markers, injuries, secrets, and plot commitments. It can also show patterns: a viewpoint absent for six chapters, a location used only once, or a threat mentioned repeatedly without a later consequence.

Those findings are descriptive. They tell you what the manuscript contains. Interpretation begins when you ask why the pattern matters. A missing viewpoint may be deliberate suspense. A forgotten wound may be a continuity error. The same observation can lead to different edits depending on genre, voice, and intent.

<Callout title="Evidence before advice">

A useful analysis separates what the pages establish from what an editor or tool recommends. You should be able to inspect the passage behind an important claim.

</Callout>

## How it differs from an editorial letter

An editorial letter argues for a reading of the book. It may discuss motivation, structure, pace, theme, market expectations, and emotional effect. Manuscript analysis supplies the evidence beneath that reading: when a goal first appears, how long a subplot disappears, or which scenes alter a relationship.

The two work well together. Analysis reduces factual uncertainty; editorial judgment decides what deserves revision. Neither replaces the other. A database of details cannot determine whether a quiet ending is satisfying, and an insightful letter can still miss a date contradiction hidden across distant chapters.

## A simple analysis workflow

1. **Name the decision.** Decide whether you are checking continuity, planning a rewrite, preparing beta readers, or rebuilding a story bible.
2. **Collect only relevant evidence.** For a timeline pass, prioritize dates, duration, travel, information, and physical recovery.
3. **Group findings by consequence.** Separate definite contradictions, plausible risks, craft opportunities, and harmless variation.
4. **Verify in context.** Read the full scenes around every high-impact finding.
5. **Convert findings into edits.** Write an action, owner, location, and reason for each accepted change.
6. **Recheck downstream scenes.** A corrected fact may affect later dialogue, motivation, or chronology.

Suppose a mystery draft introduces a brass key in chapter two, shows Mara losing it in chapter nine, and lets her unlock a cabinet with it in chapter fourteen. Analysis can surface the three mentions. You decide whether the later use is an error, a clue that someone returned the key, or an opportunity to add a recovery scene.

## What automated analysis can do well

Software is useful for scale and retrieval. It can index names, surface repeated terms, connect mentions to chapters, and make cross-manuscript searching faster. It is particularly helpful after substantial rewrites, when the author remembers several versions at once.

Automation becomes less reliable when the text is ambiguous. Sarcasm, indirect speech, unnamed characters, dreams, unreliable narration, and nonlinear time all require interpretation. Treat extracted material as a navigable first pass, not unquestionable canon. Verify consequential findings against the manuscript.

## Common mistakes

The first mistake is analyzing everything because it can be counted. Frequency alone does not measure importance. A minor object may appear twice and determine the ending; a common location may need no attention.

The second is mixing draft versions. Label the manuscript date or revision clearly, then archive stale reports. Otherwise a finding may be true of a scene that no longer exists.

The third is accepting a warning without reading context. A name change might refer to an intentional disguise. Two different dates might belong to the narrator’s lie. Evidence narrows the question; it does not eliminate reading.

The fourth is producing a report with no revision path. Every retained finding should become a decision: fix, clarify, investigate, defer, or dismiss with a reason.

## What a useful result looks like

At the end, you should have a compact map of the draft, a short list of verified risks, and an ordered set of edits. The map can feed a living story bible. The edit list can guide a continuity pass or a scene rewrite. The important measure is not the number of facts extracted. It is how quickly the analysis helps you return to the pages with a clearer question.

For a broader system that keeps those facts useful after the report is finished, read [What Is a Story Bible, and Do You Actually Need One?](/blog/what-is-a-story-bible-and-do-you-need-one).

## Before you act on the report

Choose one high-impact finding and follow it all the way from evidence to edit to verification. This small test reveals whether the analysis is specific enough to support real revision. If it cannot show where the claim came from or what uncertainty remains, improve the report before scaling the process.
