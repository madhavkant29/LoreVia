A beta reader packet gives readers everything needed to understand the assignment without explaining how they should interpret the story. Include one stable manuscript, a brief welcome, the kind of feedback you need, content notes, the deadline, response instructions, and a short questionnaire. Make the packet easy to enter and easy to leave.

## The stable manuscript

Send the exact version everyone will read. Put the title, author name, and version date in the file. Use clear chapter headings, consistent scene breaks, comfortable typography, and page numbers when useful. Remove tracked changes, private comments, and drafting placeholders.

Offer accessible formats when possible. A reader who prefers an e-reader may struggle with a large DOCX; a reader making inline notes may prefer it. Confirm the file opens before sending.

Do not update the shared manuscript during the round. If an urgent correction is necessary, tell every reader exactly what changed and preserve the original reference.

## A one-page reader brief

The brief should answer practical questions quickly:

- What is the premise and approximate length?
- What stage is this draft in?
- What kind of feedback is most useful?
- When is the response due?
- How should comments be returned?
- May the reader discuss or share the manuscript?
- Who should they contact if the file fails?

Keep the premise to a few sentences. Avoid explaining motives, themes, or twists that the manuscript should communicate.

<Callout title="Do not pre-correct the reading">

If you tell readers that a confusing choice represents grief, you lose the chance to learn whether grief is visible on the page.

</Callout>

## Content notes and consent

List material a reader needs in order to choose participation safely. Be specific enough to be useful without revealing more plot than necessary. If a topic is central or unusually graphic, say so.

Tell readers they may skip a scene, decline a question, or stop reading. Include this permission plainly. Respecting boundaries is part of a professional exchange, not an inconvenience.

If you need feedback grounded in a particular lived experience, explain the role and expectations before the reader agrees. A general beta request and a paid specialist read are not automatically the same arrangement.

## Focused questions

Use three to seven questions linked to decisions you can revise. Ask about experience before solutions:

- Where did your attention drift?
- When did you first suspect the true antagonist?
- Which decision changed your view of Lena most?
- Was any character acting on information you did not remember them learning?
- Which promise or relationship felt unresolved at the end?
- What did you expect from the midpoint that the second half did or did not deliver?

Add an open invitation for anything important the questions missed. Avoid a survey so long that it becomes a second reading assignment.

## A response method

Tell readers whether you prefer margin comments, a form, an email, a conversation, or some combination. Provide the links or files in the packet rather than sending them later.

Structured questions make patterns easier to compare. Inline notes preserve immediate reactions. A short follow-up conversation can clarify wording after the independent response is saved.

Ask readers to distinguish confusion they enjoyed from confusion that broke comprehension. Both may look like “I did not know what was happening” in a quick note, but they imply different edits.

## Timeline and expectations

State a realistic deadline, approximate reading time, and whether partial feedback is useful. Send one courteous reminder if agreed. Do not assume silence means consent to repeated follow-up.

If compensation, acknowledgment, or reciprocal reading was promised, record it. Thank readers regardless of whether you use every suggestion.

## Optional supporting material

Include a character list, map, glossary, or timeline only if an ordinary reader of the final book would receive it. Otherwise those materials can hide orientation problems you need the beta round to reveal.

You can keep a private author-side continuity sheet to interpret reports. Do not include it unless readers need it. For complex drafts, record chapter summaries and key threads internally so you can locate the scenes mentioned in feedback quickly.

## Common mistakes

Do not send several manuscript files and ask readers to determine which is current.

Do not reveal the answer you hope each question receives.

Do not request proofreading when the draft is about to undergo structural revision unless that reader explicitly chose a proofing role.

Do not collect sensitive reader information you do not need.

Do not treat disagreement as a voting contest. Look for the underlying reading experiences and decide which serve the book.

## The complete packet

A strong packet is compact: stable manuscript, considerate brief, meaningful consent information, focused questions, simple response path, and deadline. Its professionalism comes from clarity and respect, not elaborate design.
