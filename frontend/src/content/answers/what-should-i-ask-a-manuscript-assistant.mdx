Ask a manuscript assistant questions that can be answered from the draft and checked against it. The strongest prompts retrieve, compare, sequence, or trace evidence: when a fact is established, how a relationship changes, what a character knows, or where a thread disappears. Request chapter references and inspect the passages before accepting an important conclusion.

## Begin with retrieval

Retrieval questions establish trust and save search time. Try:

- Where is the red ledger first mentioned?
- Which chapters take place at the observatory?
- What names or titles are used for Sera?
- When does Dev learn that his brother survived?
- Which scenes mention the winter treaty?

These questions have observable answers. They help you learn how the assistant handles aliases, indirect references, flashbacks, and uncertain language.

## Ask for comparison

Once you can locate evidence, compare distant moments:

- How is the east gate described in chapters four and twenty-one?
- Does Imani’s injury change sides or severity?
- Compare what Sera promises Mara with what she later tells the council.
- Which established rules apply differently in the final act?

Ask the assistant to separate direct statements, implications, and contradictions. A character’s lie should not be flattened into narrator-confirmed truth.

## Trace change through scenes

For character and relationship work, ask for progression rather than a summary:

- Which scenes move Mara and Dev from suspicion to cooperation?
- What decision does Mara make in each of those scenes?
- Where does Dev pay a cost for withholding information?
- What is the last scene in which their original conflict remains active?

The resulting sequence can reveal duplicate beats or missing transitions. It cannot decide whether the emotional effect is convincing, but it can direct your rereading.

## Test time and knowledge

Many continuity problems are questions of order. Ask:

- What are the hard date anchors, and which events occur between them?
- Could this journey fit the duration established on the page?
- Who knows the map is false before chapter fourteen?
- Does any character act on the secret before learning it?
- How long has the wound had to heal?

For nonlinear fiction, request both event order and narrative order. If the answer is uncertain, preserve the uncertainty rather than forcing an exact date.

## Find promises and dropped threads

Ask the assistant to list setup with later consequences:

- Which plans are announced but never revisited?
- What objects are introduced with unusual emphasis?
- Which warnings receive a payoff?
- Which named minor characters disappear while holding relevant information?
- What questions opened in the first act remain unresolved at the ending?

Then classify the findings. Some threads are texture. Some are purposeful ambiguity. Some need a scene. Frequency does not determine importance.

## Separate evidence from advice

“Where does the pace slow?” asks for a judgment that depends on reader experience. Make it more inspectable: “Which chapters contain repeated planning without a new decision, obstacle, or consequence?” You can then read those chapters and judge pace yourself.

Advice becomes more useful after evidence is established. Ask, “Given these three repeated beats, what are several ways to differentiate their function?” Treat suggestions as possibilities, never hidden facts about the manuscript.

## Common mistakes

Do not ask, “Is my book good?” The answer cannot account for your intended audience, voice, or complete reading experience.

Do not bundle ten questions into one prompt. You will receive a broad answer that is difficult to verify. Follow one thread at a time.

Do not omit the relevant scope. Name chapters, characters, or the draft section when possible.

Do not ask a tool to invent a missing fact and then store it as canon. Clearly label proposals.

Do not accept quotation-like wording without checking the page. Summaries can compress or paraphrase.

## Build a reusable question set

Keep a small library organized by revision pass: character, relationship, timeline, continuity, plot promises, setting, and beta preparation. Adapt the names and spans for each project. Record which prompts consistently lead to verifiable, useful evidence.

The best manuscript question does not produce the most impressive answer. It helps you reach a specific passage, make a defensible decision, and return to the draft. Read [Why Chapter-Grounded Answers Matter for Fiction Revision](/blog/why-chapter-grounded-answers-matter-for-fiction-revision) for the reasoning behind that evidence-first approach.

## A compact prompt formula

When you are stuck, write four lines: the subject, the manuscript span, the relationship you want traced, and the evidence format. For example: “Across chapters three through twelve, trace when Ada’s knowledge of the ledger changes. Separate what she witnesses from what others tell her, and cite each chapter.”

Review the answer for missing contrary evidence, unclear time order, and claims that exceed their passages. Then ask one follow-up question. This deliberate sequence usually produces more useful revision material than a single request for a complete diagnosis.

Save the prompt only when its evidence consistently helps you make a real editorial decision.
