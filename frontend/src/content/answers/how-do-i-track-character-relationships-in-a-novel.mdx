Character profiles tell you who two people are. They do not reliably tell you what exists between them. A friendship may contain loyalty and resentment. A romance may contain attraction without trust. Siblings may love each other while repeating a harmful power arrangement. The relationship needs its own record because its state changes whenever either person makes a consequential choice.

## Create one record for each important bond

Do not create entries for every pair in the cast. Track relationships that affect decisions, conflict, access, knowledge, or a promised emotional payoff. A protagonist and a shopkeeper may interact without needing a relationship history. The protagonist and a former mentor probably do.

Begin with five fields:

1. **Current tension:** the unresolved pressure active between them.
2. **Power:** who can set terms, withhold something, or force a choice.
3. **Trust and knowledge:** what each person will risk and what each believes about the other.
4. **Last meaningful turn:** the latest scene after which the bond cannot honestly be written in exactly the same way.
5. **Open consequence:** the later scene, promise, or decision that depends on this state.

Write specific statements. “Friends” is a label. “Nia trusts Oran with physical danger but not with her family history” is usable during revision.

## Record turns, not every conversation

A relationship turn is an action or revelation that changes the available behaviour. It might be a confession, refusal, rescue, public defence, humiliation, boundary, betrayal, or costly act of care.

For every turn, note the chapter and answer:

- What state did they enter with?
- What pressure appeared?
- What choice did each person make?
- What becomes possible or impossible afterward?

An emotional scene may be powerful without changing the relationship. That is fine. The tracker should remain selective enough that you can read the whole arc quickly.

Imagine rival investigators Cel and Arun. Chapter 3 establishes professional contempt. Chapter 7 shows Arun quietly protecting Cel’s source. Chapter 12 reveals that Cel took credit for his discovery. Chapter 16 forces them to cooperate publicly. Those four turns provide a revision spine. If Chapter 7 is removed, the later cooperation may lose the first evidence that respect could exist.

## Preserve both interpretations

Relationships are not perfectly shared facts. Cel may believe the Chapter 16 cooperation repairs the betrayal. Arun may see it as temporary necessity. Record each person’s interpretation and what they are unwilling to say.

This prevents accidental emotional agreement. During revision, explanatory dialogue can make both characters understand the conflict with identical clarity even when the story depends on misreading.

A simple two-column note works:

| Cel believes | Arun believes |
| --- | --- |
| Cooperation proves respect | Cooperation protects the case |
| The credit dispute is finished | The public harm remains unpaid |

The next scene should follow those distinct beliefs.

## Track a few dimensions separately

Trust, intimacy, power, obligation, and knowledge do not always rise together. A political marriage may increase obligation while intimacy remains low. A betrayal can destroy trust while giving both people a more accurate understanding of each other.

You do not need numerical scores. Write the current state in plain language and update it only after a real turn. The separate dimensions help you avoid a false conclusion such as “the relationship improved” when one part improved and another became more dangerous.

## Link every major change to a scene

Add a chapter reference and a short description of the evidence: “Ch. 12 — Cel accepts praise without naming Arun.” The reference keeps the tracker answerable to the manuscript.

After moving or deleting a scene, review the downstream relationship entries. A later apology, kiss, refusal, or sacrifice may still assume the missing turn occurred. The tracker makes that dependency visible before readers feel the jump.

## Use the record during revision

Read the relationship turns in manuscript order without the surrounding chapters. Does the sequence escalate, repeat, or leap? Are there three scenes performing the same small thaw? Does reconciliation have a costly action, or only an explanation? Does hostility reset after every tender exchange?

Convert findings into clear actions: keep, clarify, move, combine, rewrite, or remove. Repair causes before effects. If a refusal feels too strong, check the earlier harm before softening the refusal.

## A compact relationship template

Use this for the relationships that carry the book:

- Participants and forms of address
- Public relationship / private reality
- Current active tension
- Power and leverage
- What A wants from B
- What B wants from A
- What each misunderstands
- Latest turning scene and chapter
- Current trust, intimacy, obligation, and knowledge
- Next expected consequence or payoff

Update the record after structural edits and before checking the character arc. If the sequence shows visible causes, distinct interpretations, and consequences that last, the relationship will feel remembered rather than reset between scenes.
